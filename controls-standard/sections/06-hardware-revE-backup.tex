\subsection{Panel Layout Consistency}
Where a project includes multiple panels performing a similar function (e.g., a column control panel repeated across several production lines, or system variants that share a common core), panel layout --- DIN rail arrangement, sizing, and general internal organization --- should be kept as consistent as possible across those panels, even when their I/O content differs.

\textbf{Rule of thumb:} Design to the largest/worst-case variant for that panel type, and let smaller variants carry unused space rather than designing a unique layout for each. This trades a small amount of panel real estate for a significant reduction in fabrication variability, which matters most when panels are outsourced to a panel builder --- a consistent design lets the builder run a repeatable process instead of building bespoke panels project to project.

This applies at the panel-type level, not across unrelated equipment --- the goal is consistency among panels that do the same job, not one universal panel layout for the whole system.

\subsection{Local vs.\ Remote I/O Placement}
Whether a field device is wired to local or remote I/O must be based on lifecycle cost, cable length and voltage drop, analog signal integrity, fault containment, availability, environmental and hazardous-area classification, maintenance access, network loading, spare capacity, local power, and safe behavior following a communication failure. Physical distance is one input, not the sole criterion:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Local I/O:} used when the device/area is close enough to the primary panel that direct wiring is practical.
  \item \textbf{Remote I/O:} used when distance makes direct wiring impractical (cable run length, voltage drop, install cost). A remote drop is placed near the equipment cluster it serves (e.g., near a feed or fill section) and connected back to the primary panel via the plant network.
\end{itemize}
This decision should be made per functional area (e.g., column, feed, fill) based on the facility layout, not assumed to be the same across every project. This decision cannot be made from the P\&ID alone --- it requires the system layout drawing (see Stage 4 in Section~\ref{sec:stage4}) showing physical distances between functional areas.

\section{Industrial Control Panel Design}
\subsection{Codes, Listing, and Nameplate}
The design basis must identify the adopted editions of NFPA 70, NFPA 79, and UL 508A and any local amendments. The purchase specification must state whether the completed panel must be UL Listed. Panels that are not listed must not bear language or marks implying certification. The nameplate must include the required electrical ratings, SCCR, enclosure rating, supply information, and drawing reference.

\subsection{SCCR and Circuit Protection}
The available fault current at the installation must be documented. The panel SCCR must be calculated using an approved UL 508A method and must not be less than the available fault current. Feeder and branch protection, motor protection, control-circuit protection, transformer protection, and component interrupting ratings must be coordinated and documented.

\subsection{Disconnects and Emergency Stops}
Disconnecting means must be selected and located for the machine or process and governing code. Door interlocking and provisions for qualified access must be documented. Emergency-stop functions must be risk assessed, use suitable safety-rated components and architecture where required, latch until manually reset, and prevent hazardous automatic restart. Emergency stops must not be used as routine process stops.

\subsection{Power Distribution and Segregation}
When practicable, 240/480 VAC equipment must be placed in a separate enclosure from 24 VDC and 120 VAC controls so lower-voltage equipment can be serviced without exposing personnel to higher voltage. Where separation is not practical, barriers, spacing, routing, labeling, and access controls must satisfy the applicable requirements.

Power, control, analog, safety, and communications wiring must be segregated to limit interference and common-cause failures. Shield termination, grounding, bonding, and cable-entry practices must be shown on the drawings. Protective earth must never be switched or fused.

\subsection{Control Power, Heat, and Capacity}
Power supplies and transformers must be sized for continuous load, inrush, expansion, and environmental derating. Designs should normally include at least 20 percent spare power-supply capacity and panel space unless the project basis documents otherwise. Enclosure heat load and component temperature limits must be checked at the maximum ambient temperature. Condensation, corrosive gases, dust, washdown, altitude, and ventilation/filter maintenance must be addressed.

\subsection{Conductors, Identification, and Termination}
Conductor material, insulation, temperature rating, ampacity, bend radius, and terminal capacity must be coordinated. Wire numbers must reference the drawing line on which the signal originates. Relay and contactor identifiers must reference the line containing the coil. Terminals and conductors must be identified at both ends, and externally energized circuits must be conspicuously identified.

Wire and terminal colors must be defined on the project drawings and applied consistently. The preferred ungrounded 24 VDC control conductor is blue; protective earth is green/yellow. Grounded circuit conductors, AC control, and externally energized conductors must use distinct, code-compliant identification.

\subsection{VFDs}
VFD selection must be approved by Controls and Electrical Engineering. Required features include an industrial Ethernet protocol compatible with the project, hardwired safe stop where required by risk assessment, local disconnect coordination, motor overload protection, line/load reactor or filtering evaluation, fault history, parameter backup, and documented behavior on communication loss and power restoration. The project must document harmonic, cable-length, motor-insulation, grounding, and EMC considerations.
